Christmas in Portugal: the words you'll actually use

By the teachers at Na Ponta da Língua · 3 min read

Christmas in Portugal is less about the tree and more about the table. It is also the time of year when a few well-chosen words make you sound, suddenly, like you live here — instead of like someone who has learned Portuguese from an app.

Here is what actually gets said, eaten and done.

On the table: a consoada

The main meal is on the 24th, not the 25th. It is called a consoada, and in most houses it means bacalhau — salted cod, boiled with potatoes and greens and enough olive oil to matter. Some families have polvo instead. Afterwards come the sweets: rabanadas, aletria, sonhos, filhós, and the one everyone has an opinion about, bolo-rei — a ring-shaped cake with crystallised fruit that hides two things inside: a fava (whoever finds it buys next year's cake) and a small brinde.

Useful words: a consoada, a ceia, provar, repetir, está uma delícia.

What gets said

Boas Festas works for everything — Christmas, New Year, and any awkward gap in between. On the 24th, people wish each other boa consoada. At midnight on the 31st, Bom Ano Novo — and you raise your glass with um brinde!

Two words worth having: as prendas are the presents, and o presépio is the nativity scene, which in most Portuguese homes still outranks the tree.

What gets done

Presents are opened late on the 24th, often after a missa do galo — midnight mass. At midnight on the 31st, you eat twelve passas, one per chime, and make a wish for each. And Christmas doesn't quite end on the 25th: on 6 January, Dia de Reis, there are still janeiras being sung door to door in many villages.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Learning the vocabulary and never using it out loud. Nobody expects perfect Portuguese at a family table — they expect you to try, and to know when to say está uma delícia and ask for another rabanada.
