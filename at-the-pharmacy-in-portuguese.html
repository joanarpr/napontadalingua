At the pharmacy in Portugal: the words you actually need

Na Ponta da Língua · 5 min read

Pharmacies in Portugal are everywhere, well stocked and used for small things that in other countries would send you to a doctor. That is good news for your plans and bad news for your vocabulary, because the counter conversation has its own words and they do not always match the ones in a textbook.

Here is what actually gets said, and what to listen for.

First, the two questions you will hear

«Quem é a seguir?» means who is next. In a small pharmacy there is no queue machine, so this comes from the counter.

«É com receita?» means is it with a prescription. Answer «não, é sem receita» if you are buying something simple. Answer «com receita» and hand over the paper if a doctor has written one.

If you have a prescription

A prescription is a **receita**. The doctor's paper is also called receita médica. If it is electronic, the pharmacy will ask for the number or the code: «Tem o número da receita?»

«Preciso de levantar esta receita» is what you say when you are collecting medicine already prescribed. «Levantar» is the verb for picking something up, used for medicine, tickets and parcels.

If you do not have one

You can ask for something for a specific problem without naming a product:

- «O que aconselha para a dor de cabeça?» for a headache - «Tem alguma coisa para a tosse?» for a cough - «Ando com azia. Há algo sem receita?» for heartburn

A pharmacist may say **é sem receita**, which means you can buy it without a prescription, or **só com receita**, which means you need one.

Five words that decide the conversation

**Comprimidos** are tablets and **cápsulas** are capsules. Pills in general are **pastilhas**, though in Portugal that word is used for lozenges too, so point if you can.

**Xarope** is syrup, for a cough. **Pomada** is ointment, for skin. **Gotas** are drops, for eyes or ears.

The pharmacist will ask how often you take it: «Quantas vezes ao dia?» You answer «três vezes ao dia», or «de oito em oito horas», which means every eight hours. **Ao dia** means per day. **De X em X horas** means every X hours.

The one sentence that saves you

If you are not sure the instructions were understood, say:

**«Pode escrever-me isso, por favor?»** Can you write that down for me, please.

Nobody minds, and it is the difference between taking something correctly and guessing. The dosage also comes printed on the box, but the spoken version is where the mistakes happen.

Paying and leaving

«Quer o recibo?» means do you want the receipt. Say yes if you may need to claim it or return the item. «Não é preciso» means no, that is not necessary, and it is the polite way to say no to most offers.

Payment is normally by card, and the card machine will ask a question in Portuguese: «Autoriza o pagamento?» Answer «sim» and wait for the beep. If it asks for the **PIN**, the word is the same as in English.

Then «Obrigado» from you, and «De nada» from behind the counter.

Three things worth knowing

First, pharmacy staff in Portugal will often give you something for a small problem instead of sending you to a doctor, and that is normal, not a shortcut. Second, if they think it needs a doctor they will say **isso tem de ser visto**, which means this has to be looked at. Third, if you are looking for an open pharmacy late at night, ask for **a farmácia de serviço**, the one on duty.

That last one is worth writing down. It is the phrase nobody teaches and everybody eventually needs.
