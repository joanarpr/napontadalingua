Everyday Portuguese

10 European Portuguese Expressions You'll Hear Every Day

By the teachers at Na Ponta da Língua · 3 min read

Learning Portuguese isn't just about memorising vocabulary and grammar. If you're living in Portugal, you'll quickly discover that some expressions appear everywhere — in conversations with friends, at the supermarket, at work or simply when chatting with your neighbours.

Here are 10 expressions worth knowing

  1. Olá! — Hello!

    A simple one, but you'll be using it all the time.

  2. Bom dia! — Good morning!

    In Portugal, it's common to greet people when entering a shop, café or other public space.

  3. Está tudo bem? — Is everything alright? / How are you?

    A very common way of asking how someone is doing.

  4. Se faz favor. — Please.

    You'll hear this expression frequently when asking for something.

  5. Com licença. — Excuse me.

    Useful when passing someone, getting someone's attention or entering a space.

  6. Não faz mal. — It's okay / No problem.

    A very useful expression when something goes slightly wrong.

  7. Até já! — See you soon!

    Usually used when you expect to see the person again shortly.

  8. Até logo! — See you later!

    Another everyday way of saying goodbye.

  9. Pois é. — That's right / Indeed / Well, yes...

    A wonderfully Portuguese expression that can have slightly different meanings depending on the context.

  10. Está bem. — Okay / Alright.

    You'll hear this one everywhere — and probably start saying it without even noticing!

Sounding more natural in European Portuguese

Knowing these expressions is a great start, but European Portuguese can sometimes sound quite different from the Portuguese you see written in a textbook. One reason is pronunciation. Portuguese speakers in Portugal often speak quickly and reduce unstressed vowels, which can make familiar words seem almost unrecognisable to a beginner.

You don't need to master every pronunciation rule before you start speaking. In fact, listening and repeating are some of the best ways to develop your ear. But here are a few features that are particularly useful to know:

1. Unstressed vowels can become very short

One of the most noticeable features of European Portuguese is the reduction of unstressed vowels. A vowel that looks very clear when you read it may sound much weaker when a native speaker says the word naturally. This is one reason European Portuguese can initially sound as though speakers are "swallowing" parts of words.

Try not to pronounce every vowel with the same strength. Instead, listen for the stressed syllable and allow the other vowels to become shorter and less prominent. This will make your speech sound much more natural.

2. Pay attention to the sound of s

The letter s doesn't always sound like the English s. Depending on its position and the sounds around it, it can have different pronunciations. In particular, an s at the end of a syllable is often pronounced with a sound similar to the sh in English shop in European Portuguese.

This is something you'll notice constantly when listening to native speakers, so don't worry if the pronunciation doesn't immediately match what you see on the page. Your ears will gradually start making the connection.

3. Don't pronounce every word separately

Beginners often try to pronounce Portuguese one word at a time, with a small pause between each word. Native speech doesn't usually work like that. Words connect to each other, and sounds can become shorter or change slightly in connected speech.

For example, when you hear a phrase such as Está tudo bem?, try to listen to the whole phrase rather than concentrating on each individual word. Learning expressions as complete chunks is often much easier than building every sentence word by word.

4. The Portuguese r takes practice

The Portuguese r can have different sounds depending on where it appears in a word. The sound at the beginning of a word, for example, is different from the softer r heard in many other positions.

Don't worry about getting this perfect immediately. If you're learning European Portuguese, listening to native speakers and repeating short words and phrases is much more useful than trying to force your mouth into a particular position.

5. Nasal sounds are important

Portuguese has several nasal vowels and vowel combinations. You'll hear them in very common words such as não, bem and muito. These sounds don't have exact equivalents in English, so they can take some time to become comfortable with.

A useful approach is to practise them in complete words rather than trying to isolate the sound. Listen, repeat, and pay attention to what your voice and mouth are doing.

Put the expressions into practice

One of the best ways to remember a new expression is to hear it in context. Here's a short everyday conversation that combines several of the expressions above. Imagine two people meeting at a café in the morning:

João: Bom dia!

Maria: Bom dia! Está tudo bem?

João: Está tudo bem. E contigo?

Maria: Tudo bem! Queria um café, se faz favor.

João: Claro. Com leite?

Maria: Sim, se faz favor.

João: Aqui está.

Maria: Obrigada!

João: De nada. Até já!

Maria: Até logo!

Notice how short and simple the conversation is. You don't need complicated sentences to start communicating in Portuguese. Greetings, polite expressions and everyday phrases give you the building blocks you need to interact with people straight away.

Sounding natural in European Portuguese

These expressions may seem simple, but they're an important part of sounding natural in European Portuguese. Language isn't just about knowing the right words — it's about knowing how people actually use them.

The same is true of pronunciation. You can know every word in a sentence and still find a native speaker difficult to understand if you're not familiar with the rhythm and sounds of European Portuguese. The more you listen to real conversations, the easier it becomes to recognise these patterns.

A good habit is to choose one or two expressions from this article and use them deliberately during the week. Say bom dia when you enter a café, use se faz favor when asking for something, or try pois é when the situation calls for it. Small, repeated interactions are often where language learning starts to become real communication.
